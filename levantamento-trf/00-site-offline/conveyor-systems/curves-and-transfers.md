## What Are Conveyor Curves and Transfers?

Curves and transfers are the parts of a conveyor system that make a case change direction or orientation. In TRFIntraflow they form the Directional Handling module family, with four standard modules: 90° transfers, curves, turntables and pop-up transfers. [Straight conveying](https://trfautomation.com/conveyor-systems/straight-conveying/) carries product from one point to the next, and these modules decide where that path bends, splits or turns.

Directional handling is short in length and large in effect. A direction change is where a case is least stable and where spacing is most easily lost, so engineering those points with the right module and the right controls helps keep cases flowing.

## Which Directional Handling Module Fits Your Layout?

The right module depends on the turn the layout requires, the unit being conveyed and what happens to the case immediately afterward. TRF confirms the choice for each position with your product, layout and line rates during the application review.

| Module | What it does | Typical position in the line |
|---|---|---|
| Curve | Carries the case around a bend on a continuous path | Around columns, walls and equipment, or where a line changes bay |
| 90° transfer | Moves the case from one conveyor to another at a right angle | Where two lines meet or a line branches, and at the entrance to a work station |
| Turntable | Rotates or redirects the case to a new orientation or direction | Where the orientation of the case matters or one input feeds several directions |
| Pop-up transfer | Lifts a transfer mechanism to move the case sideways, then lowers it | Where a case must leave a straight line without stopping the line behind it |

### 90° Transfers

A 90° transfer is the standard way to build an L-shaped path, to feed a line from the side and to branch a flow into a work station or a second lane. Because the case leaves one section and enters another, the design has to control the moment of transfer: the speed of both sections, the gap between cases and the position of the case as it crosses.

### Curves

Curves let a line follow the shape of the building, going around columns, walls, equipment and other lines without breaking the flow into separate sections. Their advantage is continuity: the case never changes conveyors, so there is no transfer point to manage. A long case behaves differently in a bend than a short one, so the curve is selected for the unit and the spacing the line must keep.

### Turntables

A turntable is used where orientation matters, for example when a case must present a specific face to a scanner, a labeler or a palletizer, or where one input must feed several outputs at different angles in a compact space. Sensors and the PLC detect the case, start the rotation and release it at the right moment, so operators do not have to time it by eye.

### Pop-Up Transfers

A pop-up transfer lets a single conveyor feed a second lane, a palletizing position or a station without stopping the flow behind it. It is common where several destinations share one line, and it works closely with the [merge, divert and sorting](https://trfautomation.com/conveyor-systems/merge-divert-and-sorting/) modules: the pop-up is the physical action, and the routing logic that triggers it is engineered by the same team.

## Why Do Cases Jam at Conveyor Transfers?

Cases jam at transfers when speed, spacing and timing are not engineered together with the modules on both sides. A case that arrives too close to the one ahead can collide with it, a case that arrives off-center can catch a guide, and a module that has not returned to its ready state stops the next case.

An engineered transfer follows a controlled sequence:

1. **Approach.** A straight section, usually powered roller or belt, carries the case toward the module at a controlled speed and spacing.
2. **Detection.** A sensor identifies the case as it approaches, and the PLC prepares the module.
3. **Direction change.** The curve carries the case around the bend, the transfer moves it onto the next conveyor, the turntable rotates it or the pop-up lifts and moves it sideways.
4. **Release and recovery.** The case leaves in the position and spacing the next section needs, and the module returns to its ready state in time for the next case.

Where spacing must be restored, metering and gapping from [accumulation and flow control](https://trfautomation.com/conveyor-systems/accumulation-and-flow-control/) work before and after the turn. That is why TRF designs directional modules with the system and not one by one.

## Where Do Curves and Transfers Go Between the Case Packer and the Palletizer?

Curves and transfers go wherever the path of the case has to change, and between the packaging machine and the pallet that usually means these positions:

- **After the case packer.** The first module often turns the case from the discharge direction of the machine into the direction of the main conveyor. Cases can come from [TRFRapidpack robotic case packing](https://trfautomation.com/case-packing/robotic-case-packer/), [TRFOnepack, which erects, packs and seals cases in one machine](https://trfautomation.com/case-packing/integrated-case-packer/), [TRFDroppack gravity case packing](https://trfautomation.com/case-packing/drop-packer/) or equipment already installed.
- **Around obstacles.** Curves guide the flow around columns, walls and other equipment, so the line follows the building. Combined with [elevation conveyors](https://trfautomation.com/conveyor-systems/elevation-conveyors/), the path can also use the vertical space of the plant.
- **At line junctions and branch points.** 90° transfers and pop-up transfers bring cases from several lines onto a common conveyor or send them toward more than one destination.
- **At inspection and labeling stations.** Turntables and transfers present the case to a scanner, checkweigher or labeler in the orientation the device needs.
- **Before the palletizer.** The last module sets the orientation of the case at the pick position, which affects how the [TRFCobot](https://trfautomation.com/robotic-palletizing/cobot-palletizer/), [TRFCompact](https://trfautomation.com/robotic-palletizing/compact-palletizer/), [TRFSpeed](https://trfautomation.com/robotic-palletizing/high-speed-palletizer/) or [custom palletizer](https://trfautomation.com/robotic-palletizing/custom-palletizer/) builds the pallet pattern. The right palletizer is defined by line setup and speed.

Sensors, PLC and HMI, barcode and vision interfaces, machine communication and palletizer interfaces tie every module to the machines around it, so each curve or transfer behaves as part of the line and not as a stand-alone device.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is the Difference Between a 90° Transfer and a Curve?

A curve carries the case around a bend while it stays on the same conveyor. A 90° transfer moves the case from one conveyor onto another at a right angle. Curves give continuity, with no transfer point to manage, while transfers make it possible to join, split or feed lines. TRF chooses between them based on the unit, the layout and the spacing needed.

### Which Industries Use Conveyor Curves and Transfers?

Any plant that moves packed cases, cartons, trays or totes toward a palletizer or a warehouse, especially where several packaging areas share one palletizing zone. That includes [food processing](https://trfautomation.com/industries/food-processing/), [beverage](https://trfautomation.com/industries/beverage/), [consumer packaged goods](https://trfautomation.com/industries/consumer-packaged-goods/), [protein processing](https://trfautomation.com/industries/protein-processing/), [frozen food](https://trfautomation.com/industries/frozen-food-and-ice-cream/), [pet food](https://trfautomation.com/industries/pet-food/) and [personal care](https://trfautomation.com/industries/personal-care-and-cosmetics/) manufacturers.
