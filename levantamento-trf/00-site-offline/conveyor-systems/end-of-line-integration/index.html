What is end-of-line automation?

End-of-line automation covers every step after primary packaging: case packing, case conveying and routing, palletizing, and the handoff to stretch wrapping, labeling or warehouse systems. Engineered as one system, these steps move product from the packaging line to the finished pallet under one coordinated design.

In practice, an end of line packaging system has three jobs:

  1. Pack. A top-load case packer groups packaged products, such as pouches, pillow bags, flow packs, thermoformed packages, trays, cartons, cups and tubs, and loads them into shipping cases.
  2. Move. Conveyors carry the cases, buffer them when the next machine pauses, merge lines, divert cases to the right destination and change level where the layout requires it.
  3. Palletize. A robotic palletizer builds the pallet pattern, with pallet handling and slip sheets where the project includes them, and hands the pallet to wrapping, labeling or the warehouse.

The real question is whether the three jobs are designed together. That decision shows up months later: whether the case packer keeps running during a pallet change, whether a second line can be added without rebuilding the conveyors, and who answers when the flow stops between two machines.

In hygienic food operations, the flow starts earlier. TRFCleanflow sanitary conveyors carry exposed product between processing, inspection and primary packaging, and the packaged product then enters the sequence above. One project can cover both zones as a single coordinated material-flow system.

Can a single supplier engineer, build and install the entire end of line?

Yes. One partner for end of line automation solutions means one engineering scope, one control concept and one responsibility for the result. TRF supplies case packing, conveying and palletizing as one integrated line and manages engineering, manufacturing, controls, installation and commissioning within one coordinated delivery model.

For the buying committee, the practical differences are these:

  • Engineering. Case rate, buffer size, pack pattern, pallet pattern and interfaces are calculated against each other, not machine by machine.
  • Controls. One control concept covers the machines and the conveyors between them, instead of several platforms that have to be made to communicate during startup.
  • Schedule. One supplier coordinates manufacturing, delivery and installation, with fewer handoffs between companies.
  • Responsibility. One supplier answers for how the machines perform together, instead of each supplier answering for its own machine.
  • Support. Technical support follows training and handover, so the partner that built the line stays involved after startup.

What is the difference between an integrated case packer and separate erecting, packing and sealing machines?

An integrated case packer erects, packs and seals the case in one machine, while separate machines split those three steps across different equipment, often from different suppliers. TRFOnepack is the integrated option, and complete end-of-line projects can also include case forming and closing around TRFRapidpack robotic case packing.

Case forming, loading and sealing share the same case, the same recipe and the same rate. Bought as separate machines, they bring separate change parts, separate control systems and several points of contact for one operation. In one machine, they run with one interface, fewer integration points and less floor space. TRF supplies case erecting only as part of an integrated solution, never as a stand-alone case erector, which keeps one partner responsible for the whole packaging flow.

Can new equipment integrate with the palletizer, conveyors and wrapping line you already have?

Yes, within the engineering scope of each project. TRF systems are designed for machine communication and for interfaces with conveyors, palletizers, stretch wrapping, labeling and warehouse systems. Each interface with existing equipment is defined during project engineering, including the signals exchanged at every boundary and who is responsible for each side of it.

This matters in brownfield plants, where the end of line grows in layers: a filler that stays, a conveyor run that has to be rebuilt, a palletizer that has reached its limit. A realistic integrated project starts by mapping what stays, what is replaced and where the boundaries are. From there, automated case packing and palletizing systems can be added to the line without forcing the plant to replace equipment that still does its job.

Which industries use end-of-line automation?

Plants that pack food, beverage, CPG, chemical, pharmaceutical and e-commerce products use end-of-line automation. The principles are the same everywhere, while the product, the pack pattern, the sanitation routine and the temperature change what the line looks like.

  • Protein processing: sanitary conveying for exposed product, case conveying, case packing and palletizing, including frozen areas.
  • Food processing and frozen food and ice cream: cold areas, high SKU counts and several lines meeting at one palletizing area.
  • Bakery and snacks and confectionery: flexible packages and frequent changeovers.
  • Dairy and cheese and beverage: case and tray flows from several lines consolidated before palletizing.
  • Pet food, chemicals and household and cleaning products: heavier units and dense pallet patterns.
  • Pharmaceutical and personal care and cosmetics: small products and short runs.
  • CPG, co-packers, paper and packaging and e-commerce and distribution: frequent changes in product mix.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Can an End-of-Line Project Start With One Machine and Grow Later?

Yes. Each TRF palletizing and case packing platform can operate on its own and then integrate into a complete end-of-line system, with conveying added as an engineered system when volume grows. A plant can automate case packing first and complete the line with palletizing later. Planning the full flow at the start keeps each step compatible with the next one, including floor space, buffers and controls.

Does End-of-Line Automation Make Sense for a Lower-Speed Line?

Yes. Palletizing starts at about 1 to 10 cases per minute with TRFCobot, and case packing covers applications from about 20 products per minute with TRFOnepack and TRFDroppack. Lower-speed lines often begin by removing manual case packing or hand palletizing, then connect the steps as volume grows, with each machine validated for its own application.

How Much Does an End-of-Line Automation Project Cost?

Each integrated line is quoted for its full scope, because cost depends on the platforms selected, the number of lines, line speed, case weight, pallet pattern, conveyor length and routing, pallet handling and the interfaces with existing equipment. The robotic palletizer cost page explains the cost factors, and the payback calculator estimates payback for the palletizing step.

What Information Should I Prepare Before an End-of-Line Project Review?

Prepare product dimensions, weight and packaging format for each SKU family; products per minute on each line and the number of lines; case dimensions, weight and pack pattern; pallet type, pattern and height; layout drawings with floor space, ceiling height, columns and aisles; shifts, planned growth and operating temperature; and the equipment the line must connect to.

What Support Is Available After an Integrated Line Starts Up?

Technical support and a preventive maintenance program follow training and handover in every TRF project. Because one partner supplies the case packer, the conveyors and the palletizer, you deal with one company for the whole line. Parts and Service describes technical support, spare parts kept in the United States and the TRFCare preventive maintenance program.
